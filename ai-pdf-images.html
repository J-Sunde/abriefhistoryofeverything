12 People Who Shaped Artificial Intelligence
1912 – 1954
Alan Turing
Father of Computer Science · Theoretical Foundation

Before there was a field called artificial intelligence, there was a question: can machines think? Alan Turing asked it in 1950, in a paper that remains one of the most consequential in the history of science. He was 37 years old. He had four years left to live.

Turing's answer was characteristically precise. He didn't claim machines could think — he proposed a test to sidestep the question entirely. If a machine could converse convincingly enough that a human interrogator couldn't reliably distinguish it from another human, that was good enough. He called it the Imitation Game. We still call it the Turing Test.

His earlier work was equally foundational. The theoretical "Turing Machine" — a thought experiment about computation — defined what computers could and couldn't do in principle, decades before the hardware existed to test the theory. Every computer ever built operates within the limits Turing described on paper.

He died in 1954, officially by cyanide poisoning, after being convicted of "gross indecency" for his relationship with another man and subjected to chemical castration by the British government. A royal pardon came in 2013 — fifty-nine years too late.

1927 – 2011
John McCarthy
Coined "Artificial Intelligence" · Dartmouth 1956

John McCarthy named the field. That alone would be enough for a footnote in history — but he also invented Lisp, one of the oldest programming languages still in use, and spent decades as one of the most forceful advocates for the idea that human-level machine intelligence was not just possible but imminent.

He was wrong about the timeline, consistently and spectacularly. He predicted general AI was five to fifteen years away throughout much of the 1960s, 70s, and 80s. He kept predicting it until he died in 2011. The consistency of his wrongness became its own kind of legend in the field.

What McCarthy got right was the destination. The Dartmouth Conference he organized in 1956 — a summer gathering of mathematicians and engineers to think seriously about machine intelligence — established AI as a legitimate academic discipline. Without that framing, the field might have remained scattered across mathematics, psychology, and engineering departments for decades longer.

1927 – 2016
Marvin Minsky
Co-founder, MIT AI Lab · The Great Skeptic

Marvin Minsky co-founded MIT's Artificial Intelligence Laboratory and spent sixty years at the center of the field — first as one of its great optimists, then as one of its most effective critics, and finally as a figure whose legacy was more complicated than either role suggests.

In 1969, he and Seymour Papert published Perceptrons, a mathematical analysis of the neural network models that were then the field's great hope. Their proof of the networks' fundamental limitations was correct and devastating. Funding for neural network research collapsed almost immediately. The First AI Winter had arrived.

Minsky later said he regretted how the book was interpreted — that he and Papert had meant to push the field toward better approaches, not to kill it. The distinction may be technically accurate. The effect was the same. It took twenty years and a generation of researchers who hadn't read Perceptrons to bring neural networks back.

Wikipedia ↗
b. 1947
Geoffrey Hinton
Godfather of Deep Learning · Nobel Prize 2024

Geoffrey Hinton spent twenty years being right about something the rest of the field had given up on. Since the early 1980s, he believed deep neural networks would work — that if you stacked enough layers, trained on enough data, the networks would learn to represent the world in ways no programmer could explicitly specify. Almost nobody agreed with him.

In 2006, he published the paper that cracked open the door. In 2012, his student Alex Krizhevsky built AlexNet — a deep neural network that won the ImageNet competition by a margin so large it looked like a scoring error. The slide decks across the industry were quietly updated. The age of deep learning had begun.

In 2023, Hinton left Google, where he had worked for over a decade, to spend his remaining time warning about the thing he'd helped build. He has said he now believes the risks of AI may be greater than he previously understood — a statement that carries weight precisely because of who is making it.

Key Figures — continued
b. 1960
Yann LeCun
Chief AI Scientist, Meta · Convolutional Networks

Yann LeCun invented the convolutional neural network in the late 1980s — the architecture that underlies virtually every image recognition system built in the past decade. At the time, almost nobody noticed. Neural networks were unfashionable. The architecture sat largely dormant for twenty years, waiting for the hardware and data that would make it run.

When deep learning exploded after 2012, LeCun's earlier work became foundational retroactively. He had been right for two decades before being recognized as right. He is now Chief AI Scientist at Meta, one of the most prominent researchers in the world, and one of the most vocal critics of AI doom narratives — arguing that fears of near-term superintelligence are overblown and that the field's real challenges are more mundane and more tractable.

He, Hinton, and Bengio shared the 2018 Turing Award — the field's highest honor — for their foundational contributions to deep learning. Three people who spent the 1980s and 90s being ignored, together on a stage.

b. 1966
Yoshua Bengio
Pioneer of Deep Learning · AI Safety Advocate

Yoshua Bengio has occupied two different positions in the history of AI: first as one of the people who built it, then as one of the people most urgently warning about it. The transition is not unusual among the field's founders, but Bengio has been more consistent and more specific than most.

His research contributions sit at the core of modern AI. His work on recurrent neural networks and language modeling in the 1990s and 2000s laid groundwork that the Transformer architecture would later build on. He leads Mila, the Quebec AI Institute, one of the world's leading AI research organizations.

Since around 2020, he has increasingly focused on AI safety and governance — arguing that the field is moving faster than its ability to ensure the technology is safe, and that researchers have an obligation to engage with those questions rather than leaving them to policymakers and philosophers. He has signed open letters, testified before governments, and made the case that urgency and caution are not incompatible.

Key Figures — continued
b. 1976
Demis Hassabis
CEO, Google DeepMind · Nobel Prize 2024

Demis Hassabis is, by most measures, the person who has taken AI furthest from the lab into the real world. Co-founding DeepMind in 2010, he set out to build a general-purpose AI system — not a narrow tool for a specific task, but something that could learn to do many things well. The ambition was considered audacious at the time. Google acquired DeepMind in 2014 for around $500 million, before it had a commercial product.

What followed was a series of demonstrations that reset expectations for what AI could do. AlphaGo defeated the world's best Go player in 2016. AlphaFold, released in 2020, solved the protein folding problem — predicting the three-dimensional structure of proteins from their amino acid sequences — a challenge that had stumped structural biologists for fifty years. In 2024, Hassabis and the AlphaFold team received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

He now leads Google DeepMind, overseeing Gemini and the company's broader AI strategy. He remains one of the few people in the field who has turned foundational research ambitions into Nobel-level scientific breakthroughs.

Wikipedia ↗
b. 1985
Ilya Sutskever
Co-founder, OpenAI · Safe Superintelligence

Ilya Sutskever is one of the most technically gifted researchers in the history of the field — and one of its most enigmatic figures. He co-authored AlexNet as a student under Geoffrey Hinton, helping trigger the deep learning revolution. He then co-founded OpenAI, where as Chief Scientist he oversaw the research that produced GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-4, and ChatGPT.

In November 2023, he participated in the OpenAI board's decision to fire CEO Sam Altman — a move that lasted approximately five days before Altman was reinstated, the board was reconstituted, and most of the people who had supported the firing were gone. Sutskever publicly expressed regret for his role in the episode. He left OpenAI in 2024.

He founded Safe Superintelligence shortly after — a company with a single stated goal: building superintelligent AI safely. No products. No commercial pressure. Just the problem. Whether that focus is visionary or naive may be the defining question of the next decade.

b. 1985
Sam Altman
CEO, OpenAI · ChatGPT

Sam Altman did not build the technology. He built the organization that put the technology in front of a hundred million people. That distinction matters — and in the history of AI, it may matter as much as the technical contributions.

He joined OpenAI as CEO after leading Y Combinator, where he had become one of Silicon Valley's most prominent voices. Under his leadership, OpenAI moved from a research organization with an unusual nonprofit structure to the most commercially consequential AI company in the world. The launch of ChatGPT in November 2022 — a product decision, not a research decision — was his.

In November 2023, OpenAI's board fired him, citing a loss of confidence in his candor. He was reinstated five days later after a near-total staff revolt. The board was replaced. The episode was the most dramatic corporate crisis in the short history of the AI industry, and Altman emerged from it with more power than he had going in. He is now navigating OpenAI's conversion from a nonprofit to a for-profit company — a transformation with implications for the entire field.

Wikipedia ↗
b. 1983
Dario Amodei
CEO, Anthropic · Constitutional AI

Dario Amodei left OpenAI in 2021 with his sister Daniela and several colleagues because he believed the organization was not taking AI safety seriously enough. The company they founded, Anthropic, is built around a specific thesis: that the most important problem in AI is alignment — making sure that as AI systems become more capable, they remain reliably beneficial and honest.

Anthropic's approach, Constitutional AI, attempts to bake values into the training process rather than adding them as guardrails after the fact. Claude — the AI assistant that Anthropic produces — is the commercial product of that research program. It is also, in a sense, this document's author.

Amodei is unusual among AI leaders in his willingness to articulate specific, detailed concerns about the technology he is building — including the possibility that AI systems could cause catastrophic harm if developed carelessly. He makes the case simultaneously that this risk is real and that Anthropic's approach is the right response to it. Critics note the tension between that position and running a well-funded commercial AI company. Amodei's answer is that someone is going to build this technology, and it is better to have safety-focused organizations at the frontier than to cede that ground.

b. 1971
Fei-Fei Li
Creator of ImageNet · Stanford HAI

Fei-Fei Li built the dataset that made the modern AI era possible. ImageNet — a collection of more than fourteen million labeled images organized into twenty thousand categories — took years to assemble and was, at the time, the largest labeled dataset of its kind. She organized the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge starting in 2010, inviting research teams to compete at classifying images from the dataset.

In 2012, Geoffrey Hinton's team entered AlexNet. It won by a margin that changed the field's trajectory overnight. Without ImageNet, there is no AlexNet. Without AlexNet, the deep learning revolution arrives years later, if at all. Li gave the field the ground to stand on.

She served as Chief Scientist of Google Cloud AI from 2017 to 2018, then returned to Stanford. She now leads the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI and co-founded World Labs, focused on spatial intelligence — teaching AI systems to understand and reason about three-dimensional space. She has also become one of the most prominent voices arguing that AI development must center human values, not just technical capability.

Wikipedia ↗
b. 1972
Andrew Ng
Co-founder, Google Brain · Coursera

Andrew Ng has spent his career doing two things simultaneously: advancing AI research and making sure as many people as possible understand what that research means. Both have had outsized impact.

He co-founded Google Brain in 2011 — the internal research team that became central to Google's AI capabilities and produced some of the most important work of the deep learning era. Before that, he had built one of the largest neural networks of its time at Stanford, using GPU clusters to train on unlabeled data. He was among the first to demonstrate that scale worked.

He co-founded Coursera in 2012, which has trained millions of people in machine learning and AI through online courses. His original Machine Learning course remains one of the most-taken online courses in history. Where most researchers in the field have measured their impact in citations and papers, Ng has also measured it in people who can now do the work — a constituency that has expanded the field in ways that are hard to quantify and easy to underestimate.

A (Brief) History of Artificial Intelligence
From Alan Turing's imitation game to autonomous agents entering the workforce — eighty years of machines learning to think, fail, and think again.

What follows is the written layer of this guide — a long-form narrative covering the same ground as the timeline above, but in prose. Where the chronology gives you orientation, this gives you context. The why behind the when.

Some of the most important moments in AI history are also the most counterintuitive: the winters that look, in retrospect, like necessary pressure; the papers that were ignored for years before they became foundational; the demos that looked like magic and turned out to be engineering; the engineering that looked like engineering and turned out to change everything.

The story is told in six parts, roughly corresponding to the six eras of the timeline. Each part stands alone. None of it requires prior knowledge of the field.

Part I · The Question That Started Everything
1950 — Can Machines Think?

There is a very good argument that the entire field of artificial intelligence begins with a question nobody could answer and a test nobody has quite agreed on.

In 1950, a British mathematician named Alan Turing published a paper called "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." He opened it with a deceptively simple provocation: Can machines think? Then, recognizing that "think" was too slippery a word to be useful, he immediately replaced it with a more tractable problem. He called it the Imitation Game.

The setup: a human interrogator exchanges text messages with two respondents — one human, one machine. If the interrogator cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine has passed the test. Turing didn't claim this would prove consciousness. He claimed it would be a reasonable operational definition of intelligence — and that the question of whether machines could genuinely "think" was about as useful as asking whether submarines can genuinely "swim."

Turing died in 1954 at the age of 41. He did not live to see the field he had essentially founded produce anything resembling what he imagined. The computers of his era filled entire rooms and could barely add numbers reliably.

1956 — The Field Gets Its Name

In the summer of 1956, a group of mathematicians gathered at Dartmouth College for what was formally called the Dartmouth Conference. The organizer was John McCarthy, who wanted to establish, as a legitimate academic discipline, the study of machines that could simulate human intelligence. He also wanted to name it.

The name he chose — "artificial intelligence" — was not universally loved. Some thought it was too grandiose. McCarthy thought the grandiosity was appropriate, because the goal was genuinely grand. The conference produced little of immediate consequence. But the name stuck, and McCarthy spent the next fifty years insisting that general machine intelligence was between five and fifteen years away. He was consistently wrong about the timeline and consistently right about the destination.

"The gap between what the technology was actually doing and what people believed it was doing would persist, in various forms, for the next six decades."
Part II · The Years Nobody Talks About
1969–1993 — Two Winters

Here is a section of AI history that tends to get skipped in the enthusiasm of the present moment: the field collapsed. Twice.

The First AI Winter began around 1969, when a report by Minsky and Papert demonstrated mathematical limitations of the neural network approach. Funding dried up. The promising machines of the early 1960s turned out to be good at toy problems and useless for real ones.

A partial recovery came in the 1980s in the form of "expert systems" — programs that encoded human expertise as explicit rules. These systems worked, within narrow domains. The Japanese government announced a national AI initiative. The U.S. military invested hundreds of millions. There was, briefly, a feeling that something was about to happen.

The Second AI Winter arrived in the late 1980s. The expert systems were brittle — they failed outside their prescribed rules in embarrassing ways. Maintaining them was impossibly labor-intensive. Funding collapsed again. The phrase "artificial intelligence" became something researchers avoided putting on grant proposals because it had become associated with overpromising.

1997 — A Different Kind of Intelligence

In 1997, a chess computer called Deep Blue defeated world champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match. This was considered a milestone for artificial intelligence — it demonstrated that machines could exceed human performance on specific, well-defined tasks. What Deep Blue actually demonstrated, though, was the power of brute-force computation and carefully engineered evaluation functions. It was extraordinary engineering. It was not, despite the headlines, a step toward general machine intelligence. IBM's stock went up. The chess machine was retired.

Part III · When It Actually Started Working
2006 — Hinton's Breakthrough

Geoffrey Hinton had spent twenty years being wrong about being right. Since the early 1980s, he'd believed that deep neural networks could actually work if you had enough layers and enough data. The field had mostly given up on this idea. Hinton hadn't.

In 2006, he published a paper demonstrating that deep networks could be trained effectively using a technique called pretraining. It was a narrow technical result. It reopened a door that had been effectively closed for thirty years. Hinton is now called the "Godfather of Deep Learning." In 2023, he left Google to spend his remaining time warning about the thing he'd helped create.

2012 — The Moment Everything Changed

ImageNet was a project that Fei-Fei Li had spent years building: a database of more than a million labeled images, organized into a thousand categories. Starting in 2010, she organized an annual competition asking research teams to build systems that could correctly classify images from the dataset.

In 2012, a team from the University of Toronto — Geoffrey Hinton and two of his students — entered a deep neural network they called AlexNet. It didn't just win. It won by a margin so large it looked like an error in the scoring. Previous approaches were achieving error rates around 26%. AlexNet achieved 15.3%.

Every serious AI researcher in the world understood what had happened. The slide decks that didn't mention neural networks were quietly updated. The labs that understood what AlexNet implied — Google, Facebook, Baidu — began recruiting researchers at a pace the academic world had never seen.

"The winter was over."
2014–2016 — Three More Dominoes

In 2014, Ian Goodfellow invented Generative Adversarial Networks — GANs — in a single night, reportedly after a bar argument. The idea: pit two neural networks against each other. One generates fake images. One tries to detect fakes. Both get better through competition. The immediate reaction was fascination. The ten-year consequence was that the concept of a trustworthy photograph was permanently complicated.

In 2016, Google DeepMind's AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol, one of the world's best Go players, in a five-game match. Unlike chess, Go has more possible positions than atoms in the observable universe. Nobody thought a machine would be competitive at top-level Go for at least another decade. AlphaGo won in four games. Lee Sedol retired from competitive Go in 2019, citing the impossibility of competing against AI.

Part IV · The Architecture That Ate Everything
2017 — "Attention Is All You Need"

In 2017, a team of eight researchers at Google published a paper with that title. The paper introduced the Transformer architecture — a new way of building neural networks that was particularly good at processing sequences of data, which is to say: language.

Previous approaches to language modeling required networks to process text sequentially, word by word. The Transformer used a mechanism called "attention" that allowed the network to consider all parts of a sequence simultaneously, weighting each element by its relevance to every other. The technical description is easier to understand through its consequences: every large language model you have interacted with is a descendant of this architecture.

2018–2020 — Scale as a Strategy

The years following the Transformer paper were characterized by a single, somewhat counterintuitive insight: bigger worked better. Not just a little better. Dramatically better, in ways that weren't fully explained by existing theory.

BERT and GPT-1 arrived in 2018 — the first generation of large language models built on the Transformer. GPT-3, released in 2020, had 175 billion parameters — roughly 100 times the scale of its predecessor — and exhibited capabilities that surprised even its creators. It could write coherent essays. It could complete code. It could translate between languages it hadn't been explicitly trained to translate. The researchers called these emergent capabilities — abilities that appeared at scale without being explicitly programmed. The practical response was to scale further.

Part V · When Everyone Got Involved
November 2022 — ChatGPT

On November 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT as a free public research preview. One hundred million people signed up in the first two months — the fastest consumer product adoption in history to that point. The servers were overwhelmed. The response went beyond technology coverage into mainstream news, dinner table conversation, and emergency school board meetings about plagiarism.

The companies that weren't OpenAI spent the following months in a state that politely might be called "accelerated prioritization." Google had been sitting on technology comparable to ChatGPT for years, having decided it was too risky to release. The competitive pressure changed that calculation. Microsoft invested $10 billion in OpenAI. Google rushed Bard to market, made a factual error in the demo video, and watched $100 billion of market capitalization disappear in a single afternoon.

2023 — The Proliferation

The twelve months following ChatGPT saw the release of more consequential AI systems than the previous decade combined. Anthropic released Claude. Google released Gemini. Meta released Llama as open source — meaning any researcher or company could build on it without restriction. Voice synthesis reached a quality where audio clips of anyone saying anything became trivially producible. AI coding assistants moved from novelty to standard practice among software engineers.

The question that had animated the field since Turing — can machines think? — had been quietly replaced by a different and more practical one: what, specifically, are they going to do to our economy?

2024 — Agents and Reasoning

The 2024 wave of AI development was characterized by two advances. The first was reasoning — models that could work through complex problems step by step, checking their own work, rather than producing a single fluent output. OpenAI's o1 and its successors demonstrated that models trained to "think before answering" substantially outperformed their predecessors on mathematics, coding, and logical reasoning tasks.

The second was agency. The combination of capable language models with the ability to use tools — browse the web, write and execute code, interact with software — produced "agents" that could complete multi-step tasks without human guidance at each stage. By the end of 2024, AI systems could meaningfully automate tasks that had previously required sustained human attention: research, coding projects, document analysis, scheduling. The economic implications were no longer theoretical.

Part VI · Where This Goes

The honest answer to "where does AI go from here" is that nobody knows, including the people building it. But the forecasts made with reasonable confidence tend to cluster around a few themes.

In the near term — the next two to three years — AI agents will enter the workforce in a more systematic way. Not replacing entire jobs at first, but automating components of knowledge work: drafting, research, scheduling, code review, customer service. The productivity gains for firms that adopt this effectively will be significant. The displacement effects for workers whose jobs consist primarily of those tasks will be real.

Multimodal capability — the ability to seamlessly integrate text, image, audio, and video — will become standard rather than remarkable. The systems of 2027 will take in a video of a malfunctioning machine and produce a repair guide as routinely as current systems summarize documents.

Scientific discovery — the application that arguably has the most immediately meaningful stakes — is accelerating. AlphaFold's protein structure work was one example. AI systems contributing meaningfully to drug discovery, materials science, and climate modeling appear likely within the decade, though the specifics are genuinely uncertain.

The harder questions — whether AI systems will reach or surpass general human intelligence, what that would mean, how society would reorganize around it — remain genuinely contested. The people building the systems disagree. The people studying the risks disagree. The philosophers disagree, largely because they've been disagreeing about the relevant definitions since before the computers existed.

What isn't seriously contested is that the rate of change is higher than it has ever been, and that the systems of 2030 will look as different from ChatGPT as ChatGPT looks from ELIZA. In 1966, ELIZA fooled people into thinking they were having a meaningful conversation. In 2022, ChatGPT had a hundred million people questioning what meaningful conversation meant.

Turing asked whether machines could think. We are somewhere in the middle of finding out, and the answer so far appears to be: it depends on what you mean by think — which was more or less what he suspected.
